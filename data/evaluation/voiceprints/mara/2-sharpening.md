I used to think sharpening was a chore you did before the real work. It isn't. It's the real work — or at least half of it — because a dull chisel makes every other skill look worse than it is.

My setup is boring on purpose. Two diamond plates, coarse and fine, a leather strop with green compound, and a cheap honing guide I've had for ten years. I've tried waterstones. They're lovely. They also need flattening, soaking and a towel, and I'm not patient enough for any of that on a weeknight.

The routine takes about five minutes a blade. Flatten the back once, properly, when the tool is new — that's the painful bit, maybe half an hour on a bad chisel. After that I never touch the back again except to knock off the burr. The bevel gets a few passes on the coarse plate if it's chipped, then the fine plate until I can feel a burr along the whole edge, then twenty strokes on the strop.

How do I know it's sharp? I pare end grain on a scrap of pine. If the shaving comes off in one curl and the cut surface shines, it's done. If it crumbles, back to the plates.

The mistake I see most — and made for years — is rounding the edge by rocking the blade. Use the guide. Nobody is handing out medals for freehand sharpening, and the wood can't tell the difference.
