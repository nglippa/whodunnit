I cut my first dovetails on a Sunday afternoon and they were terrible. Gaps you could post a letter through. I kept the joint anyway — it's on the shelf above the bench — because it reminds me how far a few hundred practice cuts can take you.

Here's roughly what changed. I stopped marking with a pencil and started using a knife, so the chisel had a line to drop into. I stopped trying to split the line with the saw and started leaving it, then paring to it. And I stopped cutting the pins and tails in one go; I cut the tails, transferred them, then walked away for a coffee before touching the pins.

Speed came later. Much later. For the first year I think a single drawer took me an evening, and I'd usually find one baseline I'd chopped past. Now it's closer to an hour, and the gaps are mostly the kind you notice only because you know they're there.

Do you need dovetails? Honestly, no. A rebate and some glue will hold a drawer together for decades. But there's a moment when you tap the joint home and it closes — tight, square, no light through it — and I haven't found anything else in the workshop that feels quite like that.
