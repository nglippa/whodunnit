A heat pump does not make heat. It moves heat that is already there, which is why it can deliver three or four units of warmth for every unit of electricity it uses.

Even air at freezing point holds a surprising amount of thermal energy. The outdoor unit blows that air across a coil filled with refrigerant that boils at a very low temperature, well below zero. As the refrigerant boils it absorbs heat from the air, the same way sweat cools your skin as it evaporates. The fan pushes the now slightly colder air away, and the refrigerant, now a gas, travels to the compressor.

The compressor is where the electricity goes. Squeezing the gas raises its pressure and, with it, its temperature, often to 60 or 70 degrees. That hot gas flows indoors to a second coil. Room air, or water for radiators, passes over the coil and takes the heat. As the refrigerant gives up its heat it condenses back into a liquid, passes through an expansion valve that drops its pressure, and returns outside to start again.

The ratio of heat delivered to electricity used is called the coefficient of performance. It falls as the outdoor temperature falls, because the compressor has to lift the heat further. A unit that manages 4 on a mild autumn day might manage 2 on a cold January night. That is still twice as efficient as an electric heater, which is why a heat pump that is sized properly rarely needs its backup element.

Two practical consequences follow. First, heat pumps like low, steady flow temperatures, so they suit underfloor heating and larger radiators better than small ones. Second, the outdoor unit periodically runs in reverse for a few minutes to melt frost off its coil. The cloud of steam that comes with it looks alarming, but it is normal.
