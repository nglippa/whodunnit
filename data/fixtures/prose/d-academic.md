We examine whether commute length predicts self-reported sleep quality among shift workers. Using survey data from 1,240 hospital employees collected between 2019 and 2022, we estimate a series of ordinal logistic models that control for age, shift pattern and household composition. Longer commutes are associated with poorer sleep quality, but the effect is concentrated among night-shift staff, for whom each additional 15 minutes of travel corresponds to a 9% increase in the odds of reporting poor sleep. We find no comparable association for day-shift workers. These results are observational and cannot establish causation; unmeasured differences in housing costs, for example, could drive both commute length and sleep. We discuss implications for rota design and for the placement of staff accommodation.
